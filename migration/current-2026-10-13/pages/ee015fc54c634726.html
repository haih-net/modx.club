How the MODX Community Died

It is time to set the record straight—specifically, to reconstruct the complete history of how the MODX community formed, what it was made of, and how it slowly died. I will tell this from my own perspective and from my own bell tower, since I played no small part in it, and some people even blame me directly for the collapse itself. Nevertheless, this history is quite voluminous and interesting, yet it has never been gathered in one place. Moreover, if you ask ChatGPT about this, it has absolutely no clue what happened, how it was, where it came from, or where it went. But thanks to the website https://web.archive.org/, I managed to unearth a lot of things from the past, despite the original websites no longer existing. Almost all links in this article will point to it. This site might not open from Russia, so a VPN may be required. Also keep in mind that pages from it often load very slowly, so you will need to be patient.

First, a little about me. Nikolay Lanets, also known as Fi1osof. I entered IT back in July 2007, long before it became mainstream. My first 3 months were spent at an internet service provider company in Khabarovsk. But then the CTO moved to a cellular company, SkyLink, and invited me over to their billing department. There, I had to urgently master Oracle, and for almost a year I wrote a lot of SQL queries. I consider this my most important experience because databases are the foundation for everything, while everything else is just interaction interfaces.

By the way, ChatGPT hardly knows me either, and attributes some of my developments to other people. And it speaks with such confidence! By the way, this is a major danger—when a very popular information source has the ability to speak absolute untruths with confidence and bear no responsibility for it. Well, all people can do is level up their fact-checking skills.

In 2008, I quit my job to enter the TOGU Technical Institute. A couple of months later, I realized I had nothing to do there, as the technical instructors lacked a real technical base and I had nothing special to learn from them. And when in November I was given a failing grade on principle because I hadn't turned in my work on time, I said goodbye to the institute and flew to Moscow in December, right in the middle of the 2008 crisis. There, I settled in a hostel almost in the very center of the city on Chekhovskaya and went freelance. And somewhere from January 2009, I started working with MODX.

It should be noted that back then they were still called MODx (i.e., with a lowercase x), and later they made all the letters uppercase as MODX. Here and henceforth, I will write MODX for the sake of unification.

Generally speaking, back then Evolution didn't even exist yet. As I recall, the latest version before that was MODX 0.9.6.3, and only later was named MODX Evolution released, followed much later by MODX Revolution. And Revo didn't take off immediately. First, up to version 2.2 it had a lot of bugs, and I didn't see any possibility of running commercial projects on it at all. Second, the community resisted its introduction very fiercely, not wanting to switch from the lighter and more understandable Evo to the heavier and more complex Revo. Nevertheless, with the release of Revo 2.2, it became much more stable and I started building my first projects on it, both personal and commercial. I liked Revo because it had a richer API, better separation of logic from templating, a system for extending models and tables, and many other things that provided great room for creativity. But this very thing later became one of the main reasons for the rapid collapse of the newly united community, and that is what I will now try to explain.

Interestingly, by 2012 the MODX community was already in decline. modx.pro and modx.im appeared only later (and I will talk about this below), and at that time there were two main communities: modx.ru and community.modx-cms.ru. Both of them were already recording a decline in user activity at that time, but still continued to hang out on the internet. And then chance played its hand. In 2012, I made a small hybrid of two systems—modLivestreet. This was a combination of MODX Revolution + LiveStreet. LiveStreet is a sort of Habr-style blog engine on a minimal budget, quite popular at the time.

By the way, its code is still on SourceForge

right here

.

According to the plan, LiveStreet inside MODX was supposed to cover the blogging and commenting functionality, because in LiveStreet that was originally the core and quite well-developed mechanism, whereas MODX had almost none of this, and the few attempts to implement such things using MODX's own means were unsuccessful.

Anyway, I published a few notes about modLivestreet on Habr. Here is one of them, and it even has a YouTube video: https://habr.com/ru/articles/157135/

And after a while, Alexey, the creator of the community.modx-cms.ru community, knocked on my PMs and offered to update his community to my engine and develop it together. As it turned out, at that time his site was running on pure LiveStreet, which was neither presentable for the MODX community (like shoemakers without shoes) nor customizable (MODX was loved precisely for the ability to do your own thing, while LiveStreet, although it had the necessary functionality out of the box, was very difficult to customize). Well, that's when I got into the big MODX community. On November 5, 2012, Maxim published the article A New Page in the History of the Russian-speaking MODX Community. And the community received this news with joy back then.

But, as the saying goes, they started with a prayer and ended with a dirge. As early as November 26, 2012, Dmi3y published the article Give Revolution to the Revolutionaries!
